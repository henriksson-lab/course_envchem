Carbonate model and reference sheet

Conditions and constants

Use 25 °C, dilute freshwater, and concentrations in mol/L. Approximate activities by concentrations, with standard-state factors implicit in the numerical equilibrium expressions. Ignore ion pairs, organic acids, phosphate, borate, and other acid–base systems. These choices are unsuitable for seawater or concentrated solutions.

Quantity Teaching value Meaning
\(pK_1\) 6.35 First dissociation of the combined dissolved carbon dioxide pool
\(pK_2\) 10.33 Bicarbonate dissociation
\(K_w\) \(1.0\times10^{-14}\) Numerical water ion product in this concentration approximation
\(K_H\) \(0.034\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,atm^{-1}}\) Dissolved carbon dioxide pool per gas partial pressure
\(K_{sp}\), calcite \(3.3\times10^{-9}\) Numerical free-ion concentration product at saturation in this approximation

These are rounded teaching constants, consistent with the carbonate, gas, and calcite entries in the USGS PHREEQC database. The database uses a thermodynamic activity convention; using its rounded values with concentrations is our dilute-water approximation. Constants change with temperature and solution conditions. Use the supplied values consistently rather than mixing constants from different conventions.

Species and equilibria

Write \(\mathrm{CO_2^*}\) for \(\mathrm{CO_2(aq)}+\mathrm{H_2CO_3}\). It is a combined pool, not the chemical formula of a single molecule. Most of this pool is dissolved molecular carbon dioxide under these conditions. The effective first dissociation incorporates hydration:

\[\mathrm{CO_2^* + H_2O \rightleftharpoons H^+ + HCO_3^-},\qquad K_1=10^{-6.35}.\]

\[\mathrm{HCO_3^- \rightleftharpoons H^+ + CO_3^{2-}},\qquad K_2=10^{-10.33}.\]

In the first expression, the water term represents the net reaction of dissolved carbon dioxide; the notation groups hydrated and unhydrated forms together.

The total dissolved inorganic carbon in this model is

\[C_T=[\mathrm{CO_2^*}]+[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]+[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}].\]

No carbon leaves this sum merely because it changes species. Mineral reactions, gas exchange, or biological transformations can change the dissolved total.

Ratios and fractions at a supplied pH

Dividing the equilibrium expressions gives

\[r_1=\frac{[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]}{[\mathrm{CO_2^*}]}= 10^{\mathrm{pH}-6.35},\qquad r_2=\frac{[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}]}{[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]}= 10^{\mathrm{pH}-10.33}.\]

The proportions of the three species are \(1:r_1:r_1r_2\). Therefore

\[\alpha_0=\frac{1}{1+r_1+r_1r_2},\quad \alpha_1=\frac{r_1}{1+r_1+r_1r_2},\quad \alpha_2=\frac{r_1r_2}{1+r_1+r_1r_2}.\]

Each \(\alpha\) is dimensionless and each concentration is \(\alpha_i C_T\). The fractions sum to one. In this model the fractions depend on pH and the constants; \(C_T\) sets the concentration scale. A distribution curve supplies possible compositions at prescribed pH values. It does not solve for pH.

Alkalinity and buffering

The carbonate-only total alkalinity is

\[A_T=[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]+2[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}] +[\mathrm{OH^-}]-[\mathrm{H^+}],\qquad [\mathrm{OH^-}]=K_w/[\mathrm{H^+}].\]

Use mol of charge equivalents per litre (eq/L); \(1\) meq/L is \(10^{-3}\) eq/L. Bicarbonate contributes one equivalent per mole, carbonate two. Real freshwater can have additional alkalinity contributions, so this expression must be checked before applying it to measurements. The USGS carbonate-speciation guidance explains the importance of other acid-neutralising species.

Alkalinity describes an acid-neutralising balance relative to the carbon dioxide reference state. pH describes hydrogen-ion activity at the present state. Local buffer capacity describes the amount of acid or base required for a small pH change under specified conditions. These are related, but are different quantities.

For a strong-acid addition \(q\) eq/L with negligible dilution and no other reaction, \(A_{T,\mathrm{after}}=A_{T,\mathrm{before}}-q\). In a closed water with no mineral or biological reactions, \(C_T\) stays constant. Added acid redistributes carbon toward \(\mathrm{CO_2^*}\). The final pH must satisfy both the carbon balance and the new alkalinity.

Gas and mineral boundaries

For water equilibrated with a large gas reservoir, use the specified convention

\[[\mathrm{CO_2^*}]=K_H p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}.\]

Here partial pressure is in atm. Applying this coefficient to total inorganic carbon would be incorrect. Gas uptake by itself changes \(C_T\) but conserves \(A_T\) in our model. At fixed alkalinity it generally lowers pH. Fixing gas pressure, pH, and \(C_T\) independently would usually overconstrain the model.

For calcite, \(\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)\rightleftharpoons Ca^{2+}+CO_3^{2-}}\). Use the free-ion concentrations to estimate

\[\Omega=\frac{[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}][\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}]}{K_{sp}}.\]

\(\Omega<1\) indicates undersaturation; dissolution is thermodynamically favoured if calcite is available. \(\Omega>1\) indicates supersaturation; precipitation can be favoured, but kinetics and nucleation can delay it. \(\Omega=1\) is saturation. Dissolving one mole of calcite adds one mole to dissolved inorganic carbon and two equivalents to alkalinity. This process is excluded from the closed acid example.
