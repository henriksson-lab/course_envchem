Colloids, surfaces, and sorption exercises

Use the values given; do not look up other constants. Predict the direction of change before calculating.

1. Double the solid

Repeat the worked example with 0.0200 kg solid, keeping all other inputs fixed. Find aqueous concentration and both inventories.

Hint

Use the full denominator, not a proportional guess.

Worked solution

Cw = 1/(1 + 100 × 0.0200) = 0.3333 mg/L. Water holds 0.3333 mg, solid 0.6667 mg. The sorbed concentration is 33.33 mg/kg and the total remains 1.00 mg.

2. A loading limit

A solid has qmax = 20.0 mg/kg and dry mass 0.0100 kg. Can it bind 1.00 mg on those sites?

Hint

Multiply capacity by dry solid mass.

Worked solution

The maximum site inventory is 0.200 mg, so 1.00 mg cannot all bind there. More solid, other retention mechanisms, or a larger aqueous pool would be required.

3. A desorption change

A new pH changes Kd in the original bottle from 100 to 10.0 L/kg. At equilibrium with the same total mass and volume, what is Cw?

Hint

Changing Kd redistributes the fixed inventory.

Worked solution

Cw = 1/(1 + 10 × 0.0100) = 0.9091 mg/L. Aqueous mass is 0.9091 mg and sorbed mass 0.09091 mg. Desorption raised the water concentration without adding total solute.

4. Removal or transport?

A filtered sample loses metal while the unfiltered downstream sample does not. How could particles explain this observation?

Hint

Compare operational fractions and whether particles settle or remain mobile.

Worked solution

Metal may bind to particles excluded by filtration while remaining in the moving water-plus-particle system. Measure particle loads, size fractions, flow, and phase inventories. A filtered concentration decrease alone does not establish destruction or permanent retention.

Explore: the two-phase bottle

Predict what happens to the water concentration if you double the solid while keeping the solute and water fixed. Then predict what a tenfold drop in Kd, for example after a pH change, does to the mass on the solid.

Reference case: 1.00 mg solute, 1.00 L water, 0.0100 kg solid and Kd = 100 L/kg give 0.500 mg/L in water and 0.500 mg on the solid.

E1. Choose Worked example, then Double the solid. Check both water concentrations by hand with Cw = MT/(Vw + Kdms). Why is the second one a third, not a quarter?

E2. Choose pH change: Kd falls to 10. How much solute moved from the solid back into the water? Was any solute added?

E3. Reset, then find the solid mass at which exactly half the solute is sorbed for Kd = 20 L/kg. Explain your answer using the idea of an “extra volume” Kdms.

Model conditions and hand check

For the worked example, Kdms = 100 × 0.0100 = 1.00 L, so Cw = 1.00/(1.00 + 1.00) = 0.500 mg/L. The water holds 0.500 mg, the solid 0.500 mg, and q = 50.0 mg/kg. With 0.0200 kg solid, Cw = 1/3.00 = 0.333 mg/L. With Kd = 10 L/kg, Cw = 1/1.10 = 0.909 mg/L.

The bottle is closed and at equilibrium. Sorption is linear (q = KdCw), so there is no capacity limit; a real solid holds at most qmax × ms. Kd is set by hand: the demo does not calculate how pH changes it. There is no degradation, loss to the walls, or competition from other solutes. The comparison curve uses a Kd ten times smaller than the current one.
